# The Ninth Record — story bible

Author document; contains the complete resolution. Not included in the public build.

## Player and promise

A curious independent researcher receives access to a conservation room. No military, laboratory, or programming expertise is assumed. The player investigates a fictional foundation’s claims through documents, observations, basic cryptography, and historical source checking. The setting combines a tangible 3D dossier desk with two authored websites.

The core question is why a catalogue claiming nine reports accounts publicly for only eight. The player’s task is to reconstruct the missing record and distinguish what it proves from what it cannot prove.

## The truth before the first clue

Aster’s TRACE project compared public historical material with a receiver log. Programme director Elias Rook wanted the public catalogue to suggest that TRACE predicted crises. He ordered reports labelled with the earliest associated milestones, while actual custody receipts remained internal. Two exhibits conflate different historical events to select earlier dates.

Conservator Mara Voss objected and retained the original receiver log, custody receipt and editorial history under Record IX. Signal technician Luca Hale warned that the recurrent ECHO marker did not establish prediction or causation. Archive custodian Anika Sen preserved the working materials and invited an independent reader to compare them.

The player establishes deliberate retrospective dating through the signed order and corroborating discrepancies. The IX content digest identifies the retained log, not the truth of a theory or an independently trusted creation time. The receiver’s recurring ECHO marker remains unexplained. The science-fiction atmosphere lives in that bounded uncertainty; it does not negate the player’s verified work.

## Characters

- **Anika Sen:** present-day custodian and patient guide. Helps players distinguish leads, evidence, and interpretation. Four letters unlock at 0, 3, 7 and 10 core findings.
- **Mara Voss:** former conservator who preserved the originals. Her handwritten warning and HTML comment are deliberately ordinary breadcrumbs. Three letters unlock at 2, 6 and 9 core findings.
- **Luca Hale:** technician who favors an accurate log over an exciting explanation. Three letters unlock at 3, 5 and 10 core findings.
- **Elias Rook:** programme director, represented by the signed retrospective-indexing order and institutional material. The player investigates his editorial instruction, not responsibility for real historical events.

## Historical frameworks

The conventional-conflict collection contains fictional evacuation notices, dispatch reconstructions, and the editorial memorandum. Nuclear diplomacy uses the distinction between the 1953 Atoms for Peace address and the IAEA’s establishment in 1957. Biological-threat reporting uses the distinction between the Biological Weapons Convention opening for signature in 1972 and entering into force in 1975.

Historical facts have primary-source links and bundled factual reference cards. Aster’s disputed labels are marked as fictional in-world claims. The game does not attribute its conspiracy to real people or institutions. Its weapons-related material concerns history, public reporting, institutions and consequences; it contains no weapon-development or operational instructions.

## Structure

1. **Arrival:** invitation mark IX; five-letter direction TRACE; compact shelf-index Sudoku (1441).
2. **Parallel research:** receiver Morse and Caesar warning; independent treaty and atomic-history checks. Optional HTML and Vigenère tasks branch off without blocking completion.
3. **Intent:** align the memorandum fragments to recover Rook’s signature and retrospective-indexing instruction.
4. **Custody:** compare the original IX sample and derivative accounts using creation history and SHA-256 identity checks.
5. **Synthesis:** nine prior core findings are solved and pinned. The board’s automatic links support a three-part report: BACKDATING / IX / ECHO.
6. **Resolution:** the manufactured prediction claim is established; the original survives; the bounded scientific question remains. There is no late publication-choice failure or secret moral penalty.

## Evidence contract

Solving collects a verified artifact. Pinning places it on the board. An edge appears automatically only when both endpoint artifacts have been solved and pinned. Edge captions explain why the connection follows. Players may arrange cards, but arrangement is never a correctness test. Pin-all and an accessible record index prevent drag-only or memory soft locks.

The final dossier explicitly states what remains to be solved or pinned. Optional records can be completed before or after the ending. Repeated actions never farm points. Three-level hints teach methods; the full-solution level affects only an optional mastery bonus.

## Presentation contract

One small, smooth 3D room with seventeen original GLB props, a locally bundled Three.js renderer, clear HTML documents, original SVG puzzle graphics, generated fictional photographs and portraits, and six local audio assets. All clues remain available through a flat archive if WebGL is unavailable. Navigation uses clickable props, visible keyboard-accessible buttons, keys 1–4, H, L, N and Escape.

The published build contains the hub, foundation catalogue, conservator’s journal, public content samples and source notes. Author solutions and this bible stay out of the build. Local case saves, notes, board layout and role progression have no account requirement. Save export includes a checksum for accidental damage detection, not authentication. The scoreboard is personal, not a disguised fictional multiplayer leaderboard.

## Playtest questions

Can a nontechnical player find the invitation, understand the verification/pinning loop, use references, and finish with hints? Can a technical player complete optional tasks without leaving the intended websites? Does every correct answer have a derivation? Do the final three conclusions follow from the accumulated evidence? Can players explain why a digest does not prove a theory? Does the ending reward their work without a surprise failure choice?

Automated tests cover state and puzzle logic, but visual and human playtesting remain separate release checks.
